## 2. Techniques observed, with evidence

### 2.1 Remzi Arpaci-Dusseau, CS537

**T1. Problem-first ("the crux") structure.** Remzi states this explicitly as the book's and the lecture's design: "each chapter tries to solve kind of one main problem which we kind of call out like what's the crux or the heart of the problem and then build towards answering that ... if you're not careful with computer systems it becomes just like a bunch of stuff that systems do without any real problem that you're solving" (homework session `fin5-82L-r8` [18:18]). And: "many times if you simply understand the problem you'll be able to derive the solution it's knowing the problem is the hard part" ([18:48]).

**T2. Strawman, then numbered problems, then fix each in order.** Day 1 introduces "direct execution ... the not limited version" ([24:25]) and then: "there are three problems and this is really all we're gonna talk about today" ([29:35]) -- (1) "what if a ... wants to do something restrictive", (2) "what if the OS wants to stop A and then run B ... how does the OS regain control", (3) "what if ... our process does something that is slow" ([30:17]-[32:16]). The rest of the lecture answers them in that order, and he closes each: "so we've kind of answered this first question let's go back to the first question" ([57:33]). Only *after* the strawman fails does the mechanism get a name (see T4).

**T3. Assumption ledger that is relaxed step by step.** Scheduling opens: "we'll make a bunch of assumptions and then we'll try to remove the assumptions as we go to make a more general purpose ... scheduling policy" (`Q09UgVfragU` [31:23]). Assumptions listed (all arrive together, CPU only, equal length, known length, turnaround is the only metric), each flagged "clearly not realistic" ([31:54]). Then: "let's go back to our assumptions and start to relax them ... the first thing we're going to relax is that all jobs have the same length" ([40:43]) which yields SJF; "we assume that all the jobs arrive at the same time but they might not so let's relax that ... we need to cross it out" ([49:12]) which yields STCF; "we're relaxing the assumption ... that the turnaround time was the only metric" ([54:42]) which yields response time and RR.

**T4. Define the behaviour first, name it second.** Process: "people say we need a term for this thing of a running program let's call it a process ... a program is just this lifeless binary ... we transform it through the operating system turns it into a running program we call that a process" (Day 1 [10:37]-[11:07]). Timesharing: he describes giving A a little time, then B, then "that's called time sharing" ([8:35]). Limited direct execution: he admits "finally we just kind of named it" ([21:28]) and then unpacks each word: "the direct execution part of this directly pertains to efficiency" ([22:04]); "that's where the limited comes up this ... comes from the side of security" ([23:13]). Context switch: after the timeline of A being interrupted and B resuming, "this is such a well known thing ... it has a name that you'll hear it's called a context switch" (Day 2 [41:05]). Even for a name that is self-explanatory he checks: "multi-level page table ... it sounds like a page table with multiple levels that's what it is" (`ggPkFxOTwHY` [26:25]).

**T5. Terminology hygiene: declare synonyms, and distinguish near-synonyms.** "I'll say privilege mode and I'll mean the same exact thing as kernel mode and I'll say unprivileged mode or restricted mode and I'll mean user mode all those things are the same" (Day 2 [18:05]); "I'll use the kernel ... interchangeably with the operating system" (Day 1 [37:33]); "handlers are just code it just means like a function" (Day 2 [19:26]); "mutex or a lock and I'll say those things interchangeably" (locks [15:00]). Where the words differ in meaning he separates them: "a trap as being explicit something that's caused by an instruction ... interrupts to me are things that happen between instructions" (Day 1 [53:34]).

**T6. Adversarial framing ("what would a mean program do?").** "it's gonna be good to get in the mindset of being a mean person because sometimes we have to think adversarially about the operating system" (scheduling [41:27]). The running villain is `while(1);`: "you're really funny programmer and you wrote this while one ... on some early operating systems even like Mac OS 9 ... it would just run" (Day 1 [59:50]), and the restricted jump target is motivated by the attack: "imagine if you jump to a piece of code that's right after the part where they check can you do this" ([45:02]). For locks: "think about this like the evil scheduler that's trying to get something bad" (locks [27:11]), followed by a student exercise to find the interleaving that breaks the naive spin lock ([27:54]-[31:50]).

**T7. Analogies, kept short and used at the moment of need.** SJF: "grocery store with the ... ten items or less ... you're behind them you get angry you wish you had gone over to the ten items or less" (scheduling [48:22]). FIFO: "at a restaurant you expect there to be a fight for ordering when someone tries to break the FIFO order" ([37:51]). Sandbox: "creating a kind of a cage around sandbox ... where the running program can't do whatever it wants" (Day 1 [23:46]). fork/wait/sleep: "just like children do actually do in the real world right when they're born they sleep for a long time then they wake up and they scream and then the parents done waiting" (shell lecture [32:36]); fork itself: "one process enters two processes leave" ([25:09]). Scale: "10 milliseconds is forever" for the CPU (scheduling [15:32]). The course itself: "we're kind of like the opposite of a magic show ... we take the wonder of stuff ... and we explain exactly how it works" (Day 1 [27:42]).

**T8. Running example with named actors and drawn timelines.** Processes are always A and B, with roles: "there's a mean program called a ... poor B wants to run and it doesn't get to" (Day 2 [38:32]). Every mechanism is put on a timeline: "let's think about that a little bit on a timeline so we're running along with program a ... we trap into the OS ... the OS executes a return from trap" (Day 1 [48:38]); scheduling uses a Gantt chart and computes numbers aloud: "turnaround time of a it's 100 ... B is 200 ... C is 300 ... the average turnaround ... 200" (scheduling [40:11]). Metrics are defined before any policy is compared: "turnaround time it's simply time that the job ended minus the time that it arrived" ([35:47]); response time is defined and the definition justified against a sloppier one ([55:56]-[56:41]).

**T9. Socratic questions that let a wrong answer surface.** "what else is a program changing other than memory when it's running ... registers right" (Day 1 [13:25]); "how do we actually start like a first user program ... it's return from trap ... you just returned from it" (Day 2 [35:43]-[36:28]); "spend one minute and convince yourself there's something wrong with this code" (locks [27:54]). A wrong claim is corrected on the spot: "you said when multiple CPUs are running you can't prevent the situation that's not correct ... exchange is guaranteed by the hardware across even multiple processors" (locks [52:52]).

**T10. Explicit deferral instead of premature definition.** Zombie: "there's a state called the zombie state ... we'll see where the zombie state occurs when we [do] process scheduling" (Day 2 [19:43]); priority inversion: "that's called priority inversion ... we'll talk about that eventually but we won't talk about that right away" (locks [19:52]). He also licenses the reader to defer: "if something doesn't make sense ignore it for a while and then come back to it later" (Day 2 [37:26]).

**T11. Mechanism before policy, with the reason stated.** "we do a lot of focus on mechanism because mechanism honestly is the hard part ... the policy that does that is relatively easy" (Day 1 [19:23]); and the scheduling lecture begins only after "we have the conceptual understanding of that mechanism ... this is the mechanism the how but now ... the what what should we do" ([29:41]).

**T12. Consequence-of-failure sentences.** Almost every rule is justified by what breaks without it: "if you could turn off the [timer] you could then get in that same situation" (Day 2 [40:24]); "programs that don't run can't make decisions it's fundamental and obvious" (Day 1 [59:07]); "you'd be creating nodes and not putting them in the list anywhere which of course eventually would be a memory leak" (locks [68:15]).

**T13. Worked problems with a simulator, in class.** He runs `scheduler.py -l 300,200,100 -p FIFO -c` and has students compute the answer before pressing return ("spend one minute and figure it out so you know with certainty", `fin5-82L-r8` [12:10]); then flips policy to SJF and asks again; then asks a conceptual question ("for what types of workloads does SJF deliver the same turnaround time as FIFO" [16:02]).

**T14. Close by connecting to the real world.** "I'll try to end up each kind of major section kind of connect it back from concepts to what's going on in the real world" (scheduling [63:17]), e.g., cloud schedulers care about fairness first ([62:52]).

### 2.2 MIT 6.S081 (Morris, Kaashoek)

**T15. Purposes first, each with a concrete instance.** L1 lists seven purposes (abstract, multiplex, isolate, share, control sharing, don't get in the way of performance, wide range of apps) and each gets an example: "you might be running a text editor and a compiler ... without having them interfere" ([2:48]); "if i create a file with a text editor and i'd like my compiler to read the file we definitely want to allow that kind of sharing" ([3:42]).

**T16. Design tensions named as pairs.** "efficient vs abstract/portable/general-purpose; powerful vs simple interfaces; flexible vs secure" (l-overview.txt); "features interact: `fd = open(); fork()`" -- and the video explains the interaction: "this file descriptor you opened if that's truly to be a copy ... still has to be present and usable in the child" ([19:44]).

**T17. A strict example ladder where each program adds one idea and ends with a hook.** copy.c (read/write) -> "where do file descriptors come from?" -> open.c -> fork.c -> "ok, fork lets us create a new process / how can we run a program in that process?" -> exec.c -> forkexec.c -> redirect.c -> pipe1/pipe2 -> list.c (l-overview.txt). Each is run live in xv6.

**T18. Show the raw output and read it aloud.** On the interleaved fork output: "it may look like garbage but what's actually happening is that after the fork both of these processes are running ... you can see the f from both of them and the o for both of them" ([54:25]-[55:18]). Then the exec failure path is demonstrated by editing the program to run a nonexistent command ([65:49]-[66:55]).

**T19. "Why" questions as a first-class section.** "It's worth asking 'why' about design decisions: ... Why FDs? Why not pass a filename to write()? ... Why not combine fork() and exec()?" (l-overview.txt). The last is answered by the redirect example: "echo had no idea what's going on ... only the shell knows about io redirection ... this sort of interval between fork and exec gives the shell a chance to change what the file descriptors refer to" ([76:45]-[77:43]).

**T20. Strawman OS design (L3 notes).** "Strawman design: No OS ... not conducive to multiplexing ... app forgets to give up, no other app runs ... not conducive to memory isolation" then "Unix interface conducive to OS goals: processes (instead of cores) ... memory (instead of physical memory) ... files (instead of disk blocks) ... pipes (instead of shared physical mem)" (l-os.txt). Every abstraction is paired with the raw resource it replaces.

**T21. Honest scoping.** "in xv6 [parent and child] are identical except for the return value ... in a more sophisticated operating system there are some details which we definitely don't care about" ([56:20]); "my example code doesn't check system call returns ... you should be more careful than me" ([41:11]). Future labs are foreshadowed at the point of the apparent flaw: "the copy implied by the fork is in some sense mostly wasted ... later in the course you'll actually implement ... copy on write fork" ([67:40]-[68:35]).

**T22. Closing summary that names the two takeaways.** "the interfaces are relatively simple you just pass integers ... but sort of all the functionality inside ... is relatively sophisticated ... the abstractions though individually simple combine in useful ways" ([77:43]-[78:24]).

### 2.3 MIT 6.004 (Terman) -- OCW transcripts

**T23. Define at the top, then enumerate exhaustively.** "Let's create a new abstraction called a 'process' to capture the notion of a running program. ... Each process has a 'state' that captures everything we know about its execution" followed by a four-bullet enumeration (CPU registers, address space, MMU state, I/O state) (17.2.2).

**T24. Build the mechanism from a smaller working piece.** Timesharing is reached via a time-of-day clock handler first: "Our initial goal is to use the timer interrupt to update a data value in the OS that records the current time of day" then "But what does this all have to do with timesharing? ... Aha! We have code that runs on every timer interrupt, so let's modify it so that every so often we arrange to call the OS' Scheduler() routine" (17.2.3).

**T25. Step-by-step trace with two viewpoints.** "Let's follow along as we switch from running process #0 to running process #1" ... "Unless it looks at a real-time clock, a process is unaware that occasionally its execution is suspended ... from the outside we can see that in real time, the execution path moves from process to process" (17.2.2).

**T26. Derive constraints from a concrete failure, then fix one at a time.** Producer-consumer: "What keeps the producer from putting more than N characters into the N-character buffer? Nothing. Oops" (19.2.2). Precedence constraints are given a notation ("this stylized less-than sign") and a naming symmetry: "The producer consumes spaces and produces characters. The consumer consumes characters and produces spaces" (19.2.2).

**T27. Two mental models for one primitive, plus etymology.** Semaphore as precedence recipe ("Place a call to signal(s) at the start of the arrow ... wait(s) at the end of the arrow") and as "a management tool for a shared pool of K resources"; the P/V names "are derived from the Dutch words for 'test' and 'increase'" (19.2.2). Promise of examples before formalism: "In a moment, we'll see some concrete examples that will make this clear."

Terman is precise and cumulative but uses few everyday analogies ("power-cycle the hardware (the ultimate interrupt!)" and "we borrow a few CPU cycles" are the closest, 17.2.3).

### 2.4 Kurose & Ross (Computer Networks)

**T28. Announce the goals and set expectations for terminology.** "two goals here first is to give you a broad brush view ... secondly we'll be introducing a lot of vocabulary ... you can just sit back here relax look for the big picture view we'll come back to this material again" (1.1 [0:35]-[1:01]).

**T29. Two complementary views of one object.** "our first take here is going to be very much a nuts and bolts approach ... the second way we can answer this question is really by taking a more services point of view" (1.1 [4:27], [8:05]).

**T30. Analogy -> extract common features -> formal definition (definition comes last).** The "what time is it protocol" and the "question asking protocol" ([9:54]-[11:11]), a pause prompt ("pause me for a second and just think about what other human protocols"), then "what are the common things ... there are specific messages that are sent ... and there are specific actions taken", then "protocols define the format and the order of messages sent and received among network entities as well as the actions taken" ([11:36]-[12:35]).

**T31. Layering via the airline analogy, then the benefit, then the instance.** Airline steps -> "we want to think horizontally there's a function on the take-off side and a related function on the landing side that together provide ... a service" -> two advantages (reference model, modularity: "if we change how the gate agent actually functions ... that doesn't necessarily affect ticketing") -> the five Internet layers (1.5 [0:58]-[4:09]).

**T32. Flag a previously loose term and sharpen it.** "we've sort of referred to it vaguely as data ... we're going to want to be much more precise with our definitions here" (1.5 [0:32]); PDU names (message, segment, datagram, frame) are then introduced one layer at a time with the reason each header exists ([6:22]-[8:36]).

**T33. Close every unit with "what did we learn" and "coming up next."** (1.1 [12:35]; 1.5 [9:51]-[10:16]).

## 3. Ranking against the student's preferences

Preferences: intuitive, analogy-first, define-before-use, cumulative, worked-example-heavy.

1. **Remzi / CS537.** Strongest match on four of five: problem-first crux (T1), strawman-and-numbered-problems (T2), assumption ledger (T3), define-then-name (T4), everyday analogies at the point of need (T7), Gantt/timeline traces with arithmetic done aloud (T8), simulator homework in class (T13), adversarial "what breaks" framing (T6, T12). It is also the author of the student's textbook, so vocabulary matches. Caveat: the transcripts are roughly a third digression (waitlists, Bitcoin, movie polls); we should extract the *structure*, not the voice.
2. **MIT 6.S081 (Morris).** Best on cumulative worked examples: a ten-program ladder where each program adds exactly one system call and ends with a question the next program answers (T17), raw output interpreted line by line (T18), and a "why this design" list (T19, T20). Weaker on analogy; strong on honest scoping (T21). Directly xv6-relevant.
3. **Kurose & Ross.** For the Networks half this is the clear pick: analogy -> common features -> definition (T30, T31), explicit "we used this word loosely, now let's be precise" (T32), goals-and-recap framing every unit (T28, T33). Less depth per unit, but the pattern is exactly define-before-use done via analogy.

Close fourth: **6.004/Terman** -- the most disciplined define-then-enumerate (T23) and build-from-a-smaller-working-piece (T24, T26) of the set, and the best short treatment of semaphores/producer-consumer; low on analogy. 15-213's process graphs with feasible/infeasible outputs are the single best device for fork exercises and should be borrowed regardless of ranking.

## 4. Rules we could add to our guide-writing prompt

Each rule names the technique it comes from; most are phrased so a simple check (regex, first-occurrence scan) can enforce them.

1. **Open every section with a problem box, not a mechanism.** The first paragraph of a section states what goes wrong or what we cannot yet do (the "crux"); no mechanism name may appear before that box. *(Remzi T1, T2; Terman T26.)* Lint: the section's first bold term must occur after the "Problem:" box.
2. **Strawman first, then a numbered list of what breaks, then fix them in that order.** Present the naive design (direct execution, no OS, a spin loop on a plain int, an unbounded shared slot), enumerate its failures as Problem 1..n, and structure the rest of the section as one subsection per problem, closing each with "Problem k solved." *(Remzi T2; 6.S081 L3 T20; Terman T26.)*
3. **Describe before you name; bold the name at that sentence; never use it earlier.** Write the behaviour in plain words, then "This is called **X**." The bolded first occurrence of any glossary term is its first occurrence in the document. *(Remzi T4; Kurose T30; Terman T23.)* Lint: for every bold term, no earlier plain-text match.
4. **Declare synonyms once, at the definition.** If a concept has several names in the slides/textbook (kernel mode = privileged mode = supervisor mode; mutex = lock; PCB = process control block = struct proc), list them in one "also called" line at the definition and pick one to use thereafter. Near-synonyms that differ (trap vs interrupt vs exception) get a one-line contrast at that point. *(Remzi T5.)* Lint: any alias that appears must be in an "also called" line before first use.
5. **Every protection or synchronization rule gets an adversary paragraph.** Immediately after stating the rule, show the concrete program or interleaving that would exploit its absence (`while(1);` with no timer; jumping past the permission check; two threads both passing the test before the set). *(Remzi T6, T12; 15-213 infeasible outputs.)*
6. **Use a running cast, and put every mechanism on a timeline.** Name the actors once (process A = greedy, process B = well-behaved; thread 1 / thread 2) and reuse them through mechanism and policy sections. Every context switch, trap, or scheduling decision is drawn as a labelled time axis (user/kernel bands, or a Gantt chart). *(Remzi T8; Terman T25; CS162 timeline slides.)*
7. **Define the metric with a formula before comparing any two policies, then compute it on a concrete instance.** Turnaround = completion - arrival; response = first-run - arrival. The first comparison of policies is a table with job lengths, a Gantt row per policy, and the arithmetic written out (e.g., (100+200+300)/3 = 200). Optimality claims ("SJF is optimal") appear only after such a table and with the assumptions they rely on. *(Remzi T3, T8, T13.)*
8. **Keep an assumption ledger and cross items out as you relax them.** Scheduling, locks, and paging sections start with a bulleted list of simplifying assumptions labelled "unrealistic"; each later subsection begins "Relax assumption k: ..." and strikes it through. *(Remzi T3.)*
9. **End every subsection with the question the next one answers.** e.g., "fork gives us a second process -- how do we run a different program in it?"; "the consumer can no longer read an empty buffer -- what stops the producer from overrunning a full one?" *(6.S081 T17; Terman T26.)* Lint: last sentence of each subsection (except the chapter's last) ends in "?".
10. **One analogy per new abstraction, immediately followed by where it breaks.** Grocery express lane for SJF, airline layers for protocol stacks, a human "what time is it" exchange for protocols -- then one sentence on the mismatch (the express lane knows item counts; the OS does not know job lengths). Then the formal definition (rule 3). *(Remzi T7; Kurose T30, T31.)*
11. **If a term must be mentioned before its section, tag it as deferred rather than defining it inline.** Format: "zombie state (defined in section 4.3)". No parenthetical mini-definitions of future terms. *(Remzi T10.)* Lint: any glossary term used before its bold definition must be followed by "(defined in ...)".
12. **Show real output and interpret it line by line; close with a recap plus a "why this design?" list.** For fork/exec/wait, include the actual (possibly interleaved) output and a process graph with at least one feasible and one infeasible ordering explained. Each chapter ends with a three-line recap and two or three "Why not do it the other way?" questions with one-line answers (Why separate fork and exec? Why FDs rather than filenames?). *(6.S081 T18, T19, T22; 15-213 process graphs; Kurose T33.)*

## 5. Notes on confidence

Rules 1-12 are grounded in the transcripts quoted above. The Remzi evidence is strong for intro/virtualization, processes, FIFO/SJF/STCF/RR, and locks; it is absent for MLFQ, lottery/stride, semaphores and reader-writer locks, because those lectures were not located/analysed in this session. For semaphores and producer-consumer the evidence comes from Terman (6.004), whose style is precise but analogy-light, so rule 10 should be applied to that material by us rather than copied from him. Nothing here is drawn from CS144's intro lectures (only two 2013 mid-course videos were obtained) or from 6.033 (not obtainable).
